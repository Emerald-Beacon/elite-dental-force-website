Active Is Not the Same as Covered

An active policy does not tell you what a plan will pay. See what a basic eligibility check misses, plus a checklist your front desk can use today.

The patient is in the chair. The hygienist is ready. When your team checked the plan, it came back active, so everyone moved on. Then checkout arrives and the story changes: the cleaning was not due yet under the plan's frequency rules, the crown falls inside a waiting period, and the deductible was never met.

Nothing was wrong with the check. It answered the question it was asked. The problem is that "Is this patient active?" is the wrong question.

What "active" actually tells you

An active status confirms one thing: the policy exists and is in force on the date you checked. That matters. You cannot bill a plan that has ended.

But active says nothing about what the plan will pay for the treatment on today's schedule. A patient can be fully active and still have a service that is not covered yet, not covered again, or covered at a level that changes the conversation at the front desk.

The real question every office needs answered is this: What will this plan pay for the treatment we have scheduled, for this patient, on this date? Answering it takes more than a status.

Six things a basic eligibility check misses

Frequencies

Most plans limit how often a service is covered. Exams, cleanings, radiographs, fluoride, and periodontal maintenance often have their own rules. A basic check will not tell you when the plan last paid for that service or when it is eligible again. If the visit lands even slightly too early, the claim comes back unpaid.

Waiting periods

Many plans make new members wait before basic or major services are covered. A patient who just enrolled may be active today and still not eligible for the crown on next week's schedule. This shows up most with new patients and patients who recently changed employers.

Plan rules

Every plan carries rules that change what it pays. Alternate benefit or downgrade provisions, missing tooth clauses, age limits, and replacement rules are common examples. None of them appear in a simple active or inactive answer, and each one can change the patient's portion.

Category percentages

Plans pay different amounts for preventive, basic, and major services, and the way procedures are sorted into those categories varies by plan. Assuming a procedure is "basic" because it usually is can turn a clean estimate into a surprise.

Annual maximums

The annual maximum caps what the plan will pay in a benefit year. What matters at the front desk is not the maximum itself but what remains. A patient with major treatment planned needs to know whether the plan has room left before the work is scheduled, not after.

Deductibles

Deductibles determine what the patient pays before the plan starts sharing costs. You need to know the amount, whether it has been met, and which categories it applies to. Many plans waive it for preventive care and apply it to everything else.

The cost of finding out late

When these details surface in the chair, the visit stalls. The provider is waiting, the treatment conversation shifts from care to cost, and the schedule behind that patient starts to slip.

When they surface at checkout, the estimate the patient trusted turns out to be wrong. Even when your team handles it well, the patient leaves less confident in the practice than when they arrived.

When they surface after the claim is processed, the work moves into your billing team's week: corrected estimates, patient statements, follow up calls, and balances that are harder to collect the longer they sit.

None of this is a people problem. It is a timing problem. The information existed before the patient walked in. It just was not gathered, or it was gathered without the detail that mattered.

A checklist your office can use today

You do not need new software to start closing this gap. Use this checklist for every scheduled patient, ideally at least one business day before the visit.

  1. Confirm active status and the effective date. Note when coverage started. A recent start date is your cue to ask about waiting periods.
  2. Record the annual maximum and what remains. Note whether the plan runs on a calendar year or a different benefit year.
  3. Record the deductible. Capture the amount, whether it has been met, and which categories it applies to.
  4. Confirm what each category pays. Write down the plan's coverage for preventive, basic, and major services, and confirm how the scheduled procedures are categorized.
  5. Check frequencies for every scheduled procedure. Ask when the plan last paid for that service and when the patient is eligible again.
  6. Ask about waiting periods. Do this for any basic or major service on the schedule, especially for new patients.
  7. Ask about plan rules that change payment. Downgrades, missing tooth clauses, age limits, and replacement rules all belong on your list.
  8. Write down where each answer came from. A portal page, a benefits document, or a phone call with a reference number. If you cannot point to the source, treat the answer as unconfirmed.
  9. Flag what you could not confirm. A blank that is labeled as missing is useful. A blank filled in with a guess is a future problem.
  10. Share the flags before the visit. The provider and the front desk team should know about open questions before the patient is seated, not during treatment.

If that list looks like a lot of work for every patient on every day, that is because it is. This is exactly why so many offices stop at "active" and hope for the best.
